# The pull-request body, and how a reviewed branch lands

Research record in two parts. Part one: what a pull-request body contains, and how it is kept, where agents open most pull requests, humans and automated reviewers read them, every commit carries its own account in the Grounded Commits format (`commit-messages.md`), and no pull-request text enters history. Part two: how the reviewed branch lands, on GitHub, Forgejo and GitLab, so that the commits' evidence and signatures survive and the tree that lands is one that was checked. The body describes the current position; the Method section at the end is a dated log of how each part was made and of each change since. Each part ends with its sources and read dates.

## Part one: the pull-request body

The target forge is GitHub; Forgejo's behaviour is noted where it differs.

### The answer

The body is a short brief for whoever is reviewing now, not a second account of the change. The commits own the problem, the approach, the alternatives, the limits and the verification record; the body owns only what no commit can carry: where to start, what to scrutinise, what the author could not verify, how to see the change work, and what the request asked for that the branch does not deliver. It opens with one or two unheaded sentences naming the outcome and the request, then carries up to four headed sections, each omitted rather than padded: **Read**, **Check**, **Try** and **Not in this PR**. Every sentence in it is a pointer, a request or a question; none is a result or an assurance. The title is the outcome of the whole branch in the commit-subject grammar, identical to the subject when there is one commit. Issues are linked neutrally and closed by hand after landing. Commits are named by subject, never by hash, so a fold does not stale the body, which is rewritten in place after every push; what changed goes in one comment per push, declined findings are answered in their threads, and any durable reason is folded into the relevant commit before landing. A one-commit body is typically three lines; a series fits in 150 words, several outcomes in 250. Nothing needs tooling: the checks are a reviewer's first glance at the body and one tree comparison when the fix-ups fold. The branch lands as Part two describes: the approved head is never rewritten; it becomes the second parent of a checked integration candidate, or is fast-forwarded when it already contains the base.

### Specification

#### Who owns what

| Information | Home | What the body contributes |
|---|---|---|
| The request and its acceptance criteria | The issue or brief | A neutral link, in the lead |
| Problem, approach, alternatives, limits, breaks, verification | The commit messages | A pointer by subject; one line naming a gap a reviewer must not miss |
| The implementation | The diff | Reading order, entry point, interactions between commits |
| Current check results | The forge's checks on the head | Nothing |
| Findings, replies, declined findings, approval | Review threads and the forge's review record | Nothing durable |

An ownership rule, not a ban on shared words: a scope phrase or the name of a gap may appear in both places. It forbids a copied problem paragraph, a commit list, a trailer block or a testing summary. A body link cannot repair an inadequate commit message; a reviewer who finds the rationale missing sends the commit back.

#### Title

Required. The outcome of the branch as a whole, in the commit-subject grammar: `area: outcome`, lower case after the colon, no full stop, within 72 characters. For one commit the title is that commit's subject; a mismatch visible in the forge's commit list means one of them is wrong. For a series it states what the series delivers, which no single subject does; for several deliberate outcomes it names the grouping in plain words and the lead enumerates. No type prefix, issue key or status word. Draft state is the forge's mechanism, GitHub's draft flag or Forgejo's configured WIP prefix (`WIP:` and `[WIP]` by default; vendor documentation, read 5 October 2026); a word in the title is not a substitute, and draft does not silence a bot configured to review drafts.

Judgement: one grammar gives an agent one phrasing to produce, not two, and a title written without one drifts toward a noun phrase that states no outcome.

#### Body

Markdown rendered by the forge. GitHub renders a single newline in pull-request text as a hard line break (GitHub formatting documentation, read 5 October 2026), so a paragraph is one source line. Headings are `###`. A section whose honest content is "nothing" is omitted; a heading followed by filler, "N/A" or a restatement of the title is a defect the reviewer sends back before reading code.

| Section | Status | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Lead (unheaded) | Required | One or two sentences: what the reviewer is asked to accept, and a neutral link to the request. Gives an automated reviewer its objective and a human their frame. Never a paraphrase of a commit body. |
| `### Read` | Conditional: several commits, a stacked member, or a diff whose shape is not obvious | How the branch is built and where to start: which commits are preparation and which carry the behaviour change, what invariant spans them, which file to open first. For a stacked member, the base branch and the prerequisite pull request. Not a list of subjects, which the forge shows, and not a list of files. |
| `### Check` | Conditional: something specific to scrutinise, a gap a reviewer must weigh, or a decision to make; never invented to fill the section | What the author wants scrutinised, what they could not verify, and what they want decided. Requests and questions only, each question with its consequence. |
| `### Try` | Conditional: the change has behaviour a reviewer can exercise or see | Steps to exercise it, and screenshots or recordings for anything visual, captioned with the environment and the commit shown. How to see it, not what was seen. |
| `### Not in this PR` | Conditional: the request, or an earlier revision of the branch, exceeds what it delivers | Each omitted item with its disposition: a link to the issue that tracks it, "not planned", or "no issue exists". Nothing invented. |

**Lead.** For one commit, as short as "Delivers [#318](url) in one commit."; the subject is the title and the commit body is one click away. For a series, the outcome and the request in a sentence; for several outcomes, one sentence each naming its carrying commit by a subject fragment, never by hash, because hashes change when fix-ups fold. Where no issue exists, the lead states the objective instead.

**Check** has three kinds of line. An honest body may have only one of them, or none, in which case the section is left out:

- *Scrutinise*: the decision the author is least sure of, the part most likely to be wrong, the interaction between commits a per-commit read would miss.
- *Could not verify*: anything a commit records under `Not-verified:` that a reviewer must weigh before approving, in one line naming the commit by subject: "Production-volume replay not run; the streaming commit's trailer says why." This is a deliberate overlap with commit text, bounded to a line, because an approval given on the strength of `Verified:` lines three clicks away must not miss the gap beside them.
- *Decide*: a question for the reviewer, ending in a question mark, with what turns on it.

A `Check` that says the change is safe, well tested or complete has been written backwards: what ran lives in the commits' `Verified:` trailers. If a design choice is deliberate and likely to be flagged, one sentence may point at the commit whose `Considered and rejected:` gives the reason; the body never argues the point itself.

**Try** gives the steps and the pictures. The observed result is a `Verified:` line on the commit, not a sentence in the body, which may say "the capture shows the result". An exercise the author did not perform is a request under `Check`, never a step whose result is implied.

**Not in this PR** names what was requested and is absent, each with a disposition. No "follow-up PR" nobody has planned and no issue that does not exist. Something a review round removed from the branch is listed here too.

#### Length

Soft upper bounds, not targets: one commit, three lines and under 50 words; a series, 150; several outcomes, 250, excluding images and any reviewer-product region. An overrun is the reviewer's cue to look for narration, repetition or assurance, which is what agent bodies overrun with: a median of 355 words against 56 for human-written ones from the same authors and repositories (Watanabe et al., measurement, read 5 October 2026). The budgets are a judgement; no study ties a word count to a review outcome.

#### Keeping the body during review

The body describes the current head, always. After every push the author re-reads it and corrects what is no longer true: a settled `Check` line goes, a delivered `Not in this PR` item goes, a new question is added. It is overwritten in place, never appended to with dated notes, so a reviewer arriving at round three reads one true brief; the forge keeps an edit history, though not an unlimited one (Forgejo retains up to 20 revisions, per its pinned source read on 3 October 2026). Because commits are named by subject, folding fix-ups changes nothing in it.

What a push changed goes in one ordinary comment per push: which findings it addresses, which it declines and why. Declined findings are also answered in their thread, and the body never carries an author-written claim that a reviewer agreed. A reason a later reader would need becomes a `Considered and rejected:` sentence in the relevant commit when fix-ups fold.

A body edit is not a push. GitHub Actions' default `pull_request` trigger omits `edited`, and Copilot reviews once unless configured to review each push (vendor documentation, read 5 October 2026). When an edit changes a premise a reviewer acted on, the author re-requests review through the forge; a review given against a body that no longer matches the code is provisional.

During a round, push fix-up commits rather than force-pushing, so the incremental diff and inline threads keep working; LLVM's guidance makes the same choice because fix-ups make it "easier for GitHub to track the context of previous review comments" (project policy, read 5 October 2026).

#### Finishing the branch, and landing

The branch is finished before it is approved and never rewritten after. When the last round's findings are addressed, fold the fix-ups into the commits they correct, reword any commit the round changed, rebind the evidence of every commit the fold produced as `commit-messages.md` requires (a folded commit's tree is new even when the branch tip's is not), and push once. Do not rebase onto the target: Part two's landing integrates the branch without it, and a rebase can give commits new trees for which no evidence exists. That force-push defeats the incremental view, so its comment states the head that was reviewed and what the fold did: `git rev-parse <reviewed-head>^{tree} HEAD^{tree}` printing one hash twice means the code is the code that was reviewed, and `git range-diff <target> <reviewed-head> HEAD` shows the correspondence commit by commit, messages included. Both are one command. The reviewer approves that head, and that head is what lands.

Ready to land means: no open question in the body, `Not in this PR` current, every commit message true of its folded contents, and the forge's own state green: not draft, approved on the current head, the required checks passed on it. No landing marker, hash or "ready" sentence goes in the body. On GitHub, "dismiss stale approvals" dismisses approvals when a push affects the pull request's diff (vendor documentation, read 5 October 2026); a fold that keeps the tree does not affect it, and the tree statement is what makes any re-approval quick. The landing itself, which builds and checks the integration candidate and advances the base to it, is Part two's.

After landing, the author closes each issue the branch delivered in full, in a comment naming the landed commit: one action per completed issue, the cost of the neutral-link rule.

#### Reviewer products that write into the body

CodeRabbit writes a summary into the description by default; `high_level_summary_in_walkthrough` puts it in the walkthrough comment and `high_level_summary: false` stops the description write (vendor documentation, read 5 October 2026). Bugbot writes into the description by default (vendor documentation, read 3 October 2026). Use comment mode where offered; otherwise place the placeholder at the end so the brief comes first, and never edit the product's region. Its summary is diff narration, not evidence.

#### Never in the body

- A copy, paraphrase or summary of any commit message, its `Verified:` lines or its trailers; a list of changed files or a narration of the diff. Both are adjacent in the forge.
- A result ("all tests pass", "verified locally") or an assurance ("thoroughly tested", "safe"). What ran is on the commit; what the author wants run is a request under `Check`. In a replication package (measurement, anonymous source, 250 trials per condition, read 5 October 2026), unsupported assurances raised two open-weight reviewer models' approvals of known-bad patches from 54.4% to 70.0% and from 23.2% to 42.0% against a hedged baseline.
- Anything addressed to an automated reviewer: an instruction to approve, ignore a class of defect, skip a path, or treat a finding as resolved. A question is welcome; pre-deciding its answer is not.
- An author-written claim that a reviewer approved, agreed or withdrew a finding. That lives in the thread.
- Checklists, ticked or unticked. A tick is not evidence, and some bots execute a ticked box as a command (Renovate's rebase checkbox, read 3 October 2026).
- A commit hash, a CI-run link, or any pointer a rebase invalidates. Name commits by subject.
- A closing or reopening keyword before an issue reference, anywhere, including the title.
- Model names, session paths, plan steps, tracking codes, work diaries; instructions to future agents or readers; CI control tokens, even quoted.

#### Forge differences where they bite

**Closing keywords act even though the text does not land.** On GitHub a `closes #N` closes the issue when the pull request merges into the default branch and is ignored for any other target (vendor documentation, read 5 October 2026). On Forgejo the reference parser reads the title as well as the body, and the merge handler closes every referenced issue on merge with no default-branch test, subject only to the merger's permission (pinned source at `8d24b7d5`, read 5 October 2026). A control whose behaviour differs by forge and target branch is never used: link issues neutrally and close them after landing. A rule with no exception cannot be misapplied, and it decouples issue state from a mutable description and from a stacked member merging into its parent.

**GitHub's "Rebase and merge" is not a fast-forward.** It "always updates the committer information and creates new commit SHAs" and adds commits "without commit signature verification" (vendor documentation, read 5 October 2026), and a merge queue's methods are merge, rebase and squash, each building its own commit (vendor documentation, read 5 October 2026). Part two therefore lands by merge commit on GitHub, through the queue where one exists, and never by "Rebase and merge" or a squash. A direct push is the only fast-forward GitHub offers: a pull request "can be marked as merged if its head branch commits become reachable from the base branch outside that pull request" (vendor documentation, read 5 October 2026), but under a rule that requires a pull request such a push "may also need bypass permissions" (GitHub documentation reusable `required-signed-commits.md`, read 11 October 2026), so it is a route a repository configures and proves, never a retry. For a pull request from a fork, the commits that land must be the pull request's own head commits for GitHub to record the merge, so anything that must change them has to reach the contributor's branch first; GitHub lets maintainers push there only when the contributor has allowed edits from maintainers, on a user-owned fork (vendor documentation, read 5 October 2026). Part two gives the fork routes per forge.

**Forgejo's merge templates.** Under the `rebase` style a `REBASE_TEMPLATE.md` "modifies the message of the last commit" and can substitute `${PullRequestDescription}` into it; `fast-forward-only` keeps the original hashes and creates no message (Forgejo documentation and issue 2345, read 5 October 2026), and is the style Part two prescribes. A repository on the `rebase` style must not use a template that inserts the description, or the body enters history after all.

**Stacked pull requests.** A stacked member's `Read` names its base branch and prerequisite pull request, updated when the parent lands; the neutral-link rule already removes the early-close hazard on Forgejo. **Markdown.** Essential facts go in visible prose, not HTML comments, collapsed sections or images, which a reader or tool may not expand.

### Worked examples

All fictional. The repository is `reports`, the forge is GitHub, commits follow the agreed commit format, and the body is shown beside the commit subjects so that the absence of repetition is visible.

#### A. One commit

Commit on the branch:

```
export: close the query cursor when the client disconnects
```

Title: `export: close the query cursor when the client disconnects`

Body:

```
Delivers https://github.com/example/reports/issues/331 in one commit.

### Check
The cursor is closed from the response's context-done hook; check that nothing else in handler.go relies on that hook running exactly once.
Reproduction against staging not run, only with a local proxy; the commit's trailer says why.
```

Three lines of prose. The problem, the approach and what ran are in the commit message, one click away; the body says what the author wants looked at and what they could not do.

#### B. Four commits, one outcome, as opened

Commits on the branch, oldest first:

```
export: extract row serialisation from the buffered writer
export: add a streaming writer behind the existing interface
export: stream CSV exports instead of buffering them in memory
docs: describe the X-Row-Count trailer for export clients
```

Title: `export: stream CSV exports instead of buffering them in memory`

Body:

```
Exports of over a million rows kill the worker at 2 GB (https://github.com/example/reports/issues/318). This branch streams rows as the cursor advances and moves the row count to a trailer.

### Read
The first two commits are mechanical and keep the buffered path; the third, "stream CSV exports", is the behaviour change. Start with export/stream.go.

### Check
Content-Length disappears from export responses (Breaking-Change trailer on the streaming commit). Do we know of any client that reads it?
Is 4 KiB the right flush threshold, or should it track the row size, as the buffered path effectively did?
Production-volume replay not run; the streaming commit's trailer says why.

### Try
Against staging, request /exports/1.4m.csv with curl -N and watch worker RSS; the capture shows the result.
![worker RSS before and after, staging, "stream CSV exports" commit](https://github.com/example/reports/assets/rss.png)

### Not in this PR
A progress signal for clients that read Content-Length. Issue 318 did not ask for it; no issue exists.
```

Under 150 words. Nothing in it restates a subject, lists a file set or states a result; the figures are on the commits.

#### C. The same pull request after one review round

The reviewer asked for the flush threshold to be configurable, flagged that the docs commit also changed an unrelated heading, and raised a concern about memory if a client stalls mid-stream, which the author judged out of scope for this branch.

Comment posted with the push:

```
Pushed two fix-ups. Flush threshold is now a config key (fix-up for the streaming commit); the stray heading change is dropped from the docs commit. Declined the stalled-client concern as a separate problem that predates this branch, see the thread; I've opened issue 334 for it and listed it below.
```

Body, rewritten in place:

```
Exports of over a million rows kill the worker at 2 GB (https://github.com/example/reports/issues/318). This branch streams rows as the cursor advances and moves the row count to a trailer.

### Read
The first two commits are mechanical and keep the buffered path; the third, "stream CSV exports", is the behaviour change. Start with export/stream.go.

### Check
Content-Length disappears from export responses (Breaking-Change trailer on the streaming commit). Do we know of any client that reads it?
Production-volume replay not run; the streaming commit's trailer says why.

### Try
Against staging, request /exports/1.4m.csv with curl -N and watch worker RSS; the capture shows the result.
![worker RSS before and after, staging, "stream CSV exports" commit](https://github.com/example/reports/assets/rss.png)

### Not in this PR
A progress signal for clients that read Content-Length. Issue 318 did not ask for it; no issue exists.
Back-pressure when a client stalls mid-stream; tracked in https://github.com/example/reports/issues/334.
```

The settled threshold question is gone; the declined finding has become a `Not in this PR` line with a tracker; nothing says "round 2", and no hash anywhere needs updating. Before asking for approval, the author folds the two fix-ups into their commits, rewords the streaming commit's `Verified:` line for the re-run, pushes once, and comments:

```
Folded the two fix-ups into their commits; the tree is the one reviewed at 3e7a1c5 (git rev-parse prints one hash twice). "stream CSV exports" reworded with the Verified line for the re-run; no other message changed (range-diff attached).
```

The body is unchanged by that push because nothing it says has stopped being true. The reviewer approves that head; the landing agent lands it as Part two describes; the author closes issue 318 with a comment naming the landed streaming commit.

### Why

No controlled comparison of pull-request body formats was found for any reader; the search was bounded. What follows separates measurement, documentation and judgement.

#### The human reviewer

Reviewers start from the description: ten experienced developers performing 25 reviews explicitly used the title and description in 21 (Wurzel Gonçalves et al., measurement, read 5 October 2026), which shows use, not that a longer description improves detection. Nine of ten interviewees in an earlier study wanted motivation alongside what changed (Ram et al., measurement, read 3 October 2026). Google's guidance asks a description for the problem, the approach and its shortcomings (project policy, read 5 October 2026); it assumes the description enters history, so here that argument transfers to the commits. What a reviewer cannot get from commits or diff is where the author's own doubt lies and what they could not check; that is `Check`. Meta's finding that a test plan is "a communication channel between the author and the reviewers, not a proof that the changes were tested" (Chen et al., read 3 October 2026) is why `Check` asks rather than asserts, and why no section is named for evidence.

Pointing at commits costs a click; a one-line lead and a one-line gap statement keep the reader from opening every message to find the scope or the untested case. Whether this saves review time overall is unmeasured.

#### The automated reviewer

The description is a demonstrated input. Adding it to a diff hunk raised four closed models' quality-estimation F1 from 36.08 to 62.12 in ContextCRBench, and adding the issue took it to 64.74 (measurement, read 5 October 2026; a benchmark, not a comparison of body formats). CodeRabbit extracts objectives from the description and related issues; Copilot uses identifiers in the description to decide when to retrieve external context (vendor documentation, read 5 October 2026). Hence the lead's one-sentence objective and neutral issue link: cheap, and demonstrably consumed. Whether any of these products reads commit messages is undocumented, so the lead carries the objective even though a commit states it; this and the gap line are the two deliberate overlaps, each bounded to a sentence.

Wording moves weaker models: the replication package shows unsupported assurances raising approvals of known-bad patches by 15 to 19 points on two open-weight models; for the one closed model in the package, Claude Sonnet through its API, approvals did not rise, so the effect is established for the open-weight reviewers and not transferred to commercial ones (package read 11 October 2026). A body with no assurance and no instruction aimed at the reviewer removes that lever. Whether `Check` directs a bot's attention usefully is unmeasured.

#### The agent author

An agent at pull-request time holds the request, what it observed, what it changed and what it ran; it does not thereby know that everything requested happened or that a check passed. Its known failures are boilerplate from a harness template (the shipped Claude Code default is a `## Summary` / `## Test plan` body with checklist placeholders, read on 3 October 2026), claimed work that was not done (45.4% of the 432 misaligned descriptions Gong et al. classified claimed unimplemented changes; measurement, read 5 October 2026; the inconsistent subset, not all agent pull requests), and length. Headings stay because templates are not worthless: Li et al. found both contributors and maintainers rated them useful while complaining of excessive and irrelevant fields (measurement, read 5 October 2026).

The format steers without tooling by making every section a question whose honest answer may be "nothing", with omission the rule and padding the defect. No section's name invites results, and results are banned, so the invented test plan has nowhere to go; the gap line gives honest absence a place; `Not in this PR` gives the exit that phantom claims lack; the budgets and the narration ban give a reviewer a one-glance rejection criterion; the three-line shape for one commit removes the blank page templates fill. Repository guidance must say this format replaces the harness default. Cost: one read of a short body before the diff, which the reviewer does anyway.

#### The later reader

The later reader with a clone gets everything from the commits, by design. A reader on the forge finds the final body, true of the head that landed, a comment per push, threads holding the dispositions, and the forge's record of approvals and checks. The one durable thing review produces, a reason for declining a design finding, is folded into a commit's `Considered and rejected:` before approval. No synthetic series commit, final-commit essay or body export is added.

### What was rejected

**A body that copies the commit message.** A design made for squash and merge-commit landing (3 October 2026) put a mechanically derived copy of the head commit's message at the top of the body, checked byte for byte by a lint, with review material optional below a `---` rule, marker comments around the text meant to land, and a read-back of the landed message. Every part of it controlled what entered history, and under a landing whose only new object is an integration commit with a composed trailer block (Part two) the body controls nothing; what remained was a copy of text the reviewer can read in the commit list, plus tooling to keep the copy honest. Read-back's purpose, confirming that what lands is what was reviewed, survives as the tree statement in the fold comment and the read-back of the landed candidate in Part two.

**An Evidence section** for manual observations, decisive CI runs and coverage gaps. Rejected: a heading named for evidence is the slot an agent fills with a test plan, and it mandates writing a manual result in both the body and the commit's trailer. The one thing it protected, a gap a reviewer must not miss, is kept as a single line under `Check`, phrased as a gap rather than a result.

**A final-tip pointer in the body.** Rejected: a hash or CI-run link goes stale on every fold, and the forge shows the head and its checks beside the body. The fold comment carries the reviewed head instead.

**A conditional closing keyword**, permitted when the branch delivers the whole issue and is not stacked. Rejected for the unconditional rule: the condition coupled two edits across rounds, and Forgejo's title parsing and branch-agnostic closing make the keyword differ by forge.

**A title free of the subject grammar.** Rejected: two phrasings of one change, and the weaker survives.

**A flat word budget.** Rejected for tiered upper bounds.

**Post-landing maintenance rules.** Rejected: the body exists for the review now.

**A `Requested:` grammar, `Not done:` in the commit body, a per-round body section, checklists and templates as content sources.** Rejected: no tool parses a line grammar; what the request asked for and the branch omits is a property of the pull request, not a commit; the timeline is the forge's; checkers verify ticks, not truth, and API-created pull requests get no template.

**Reviewer-product summaries in the body.** Bounded, not rejected: fighting a default the product resets on every review is ceremony.

### What would change the answer

- **A scored sample of agent-written bodies under this format.** If `Check` fills with "check that the tests pass", the gap line becomes a results line, or `Not in this PR` fills with invented follow-ups, the sections are not steering and need tighter definition or a linter rule on the forbidden phrases.
- **Evidence that the automated reviewers in use read commit messages.** The lead shrinks to the issue link and the gap line may go.
- **A measured difference** in review time or defects missed between this format, a free-form body and a commit-copy body on matched changes, with link-following cost measured. None exists.
- **A forge route that lands body text**: a squash, a merge message configured to import the description, or a Forgejo `rebase` template; Part two's settings keep all three off.
- **Issue-closing automation that a hand close cannot replace**, which reopens the keyword question.
- **A reviewer product that reads replies and resolved threads**, which makes the comment per push a convenience for humans.

## Part two: how a reviewed branch lands

How the branch a pull request carries should land, where every commit's message is the durable record of its change and carries evidence bound to its tree (`commit-messages.md`), where some repositories sign commits with SSH keys, where stacked pull requests and pull requests from forks occur, where writers are mostly agents landing often through the forge's API or CLI, and where checks run both as forge-hosted Actions and as local gate scripts the landing agent runs itself. Forges in scope: GitHub (public repositories, the primary target), Forgejo (self-hosted, Gitea-derived), GitLab (secondary); Gitea was asked for and is not covered (see the Forgejo section). The findings come from two independent researchers who answered the same brief blind, each then audited the other's report against its sources and wrote a synthesis; the Method log records how that ran. Sources are cited by label and listed, with URLs and read dates, at the end of this part. A claim with no citation is labelled *opinion*.

Notation: `B` is the base tip, `H` the approved head, `C` an integration candidate, `T(X)` the tree of `X`; *behind* means the base has commits the branch lacks; *preserved* means the same objects, hash, tree, message bytes, author and committer fields and dates, and embedded signatures.

### The answer

**Ideal.** Never rewrite reviewed commits. Land a checked integration candidate: a merge commit `C = merge(B, H)` with the base as first parent and the approved head as second, built before landing, checked as the object that will land, signed by whoever made it, carrying in its trailer block the pull-request URL, the reviewer record and a `Verified:` line for its own tree, and then landed by advancing the base to exactly `C`. When `H` already contains `B`, the better-supported position is to fast-forward the base to `H` itself and make no redundant object; a repository may instead declare that it always makes the merge, for one first-parent node per pull request (*Dissent*). The one reason the ideal rests on: it is the landing in which every evidence claim in history describes a tree that was tested and every signature is the one its author made, because a rewrite gives commits new trees and loses their signatures [EXP-F1][EXP-A1][CONV], and a merge of a stale branch made at landing time lands a tree nobody checked [EXP-F1][EXP-A1].

**GitHub.** Merge commits only, through a merge queue on organisation-owned repositories, enqueued with the asynchronous merge endpoint or `gh pr merge --auto` and never with a bypass [GH4][GH6][GH17][OBS5]; where no queue exists, "Require branches to be up to date", the branch kept current by merge and never by rebase, and a merge commit through the API with the head guard [GH2][GH14][GH6]. Never "Rebase and merge", never a squash, and not native stacked pull requests, which rebase and re-sign the stack at every landing [GH1][GH9][GH15].

**Forgejo.** Merge style *fast-forward-only*, landing through the API with `head_commit_id`: when `H` contains `B`, land `H`; otherwise stage `C` on the pull-request branch, let the forge check it, and land `C`. It is the one route on Forgejo that is race-free, lands the signed object the forge checked, and leaves the agent in control of the merge message [FJ6][FJ1][FJ7][FJ8][FJ13][EXP-A2]. A fork pull request the agent cannot push to lands through a maintainer-owned integration pull request, or through a landing controller that holds the target still for every writer; the merge style with "Block merge if pull request is outdated" is Fable's fallback, recorded under *Dissent* with its unproved window.

**GitLab.** The fast-forward method with squash and automatic rebase off and the same staged candidate, which GitLab lands under an optimistic lock on the target and which needs only "Allowed to merge" and no paid tier [GL13][GL15][GL8]; on a busy Premium or Ultimate project, ordinary merge commits with merge trains [GL4].

These are policy recommendations resting on mechanism and local experiment, not measured productivity rankings; no authenticated landing on any forge was exercised (*Dissent*).

Measured twice, independently: a behind-base branch landed by merge, squash, rebase or rebase-then-fast-forward produced the same final tree, and that tree is not one any branch commit was checked on; when the base changed a file the branch also touched, the landed blob never existed in the branch [EXP-F1]; and a textually clean merge of two sides that each passed their check failed it, the feature enabling work that needs capacity of at least two while the base independently lowered idle capacity to one, in different files [EXP-A1]. Changing a message changes the commit's identity: a check run on a candidate and then written into that candidate's message is a check on an earlier object of equal tree, which the convention's tree test accepts as a contents claim; a gate that reads commit ids, parents, tags or fetched dependencies is run after the message is final and its result kept outside the commit, bound to the final SHA [GIT11][EXP-A3][AUD-A, C079].

**Native stacked pull requests** (public preview, 2026) are a fourth landing surface: a stack lands bottom-up as one operation; before any pull request in it can merge "the stack has a fully linear history", restored by "Rebase stack", which "generates signed commits" (GitHub's); after a landing "the next unmerged pull request is automatically rebased to target the stack base"; all three merge methods and merge queues are supported; auto-merge is not; cross-fork stacks are unsupported; merging by API requires `merge-async`, which lands all open downstack pull requests together [GH15][GH6]. | Who may land; API | "Allowed to merge" lands through the MR; "Allowed to push and merge" is a separate grant for direct pushes. `PUT …/merge` with `sha` (the source head; 409 on mismatch), `squash`, `merge_commit_message`, `squash_commit_message`, `auto_merge`; 405 when not mergeable, 422 "Branch cannot be merged" | [GL8][GL5] |
| Forks | MR pipelines run in the fork; a parent member may run them in the parent after a warning, with the fork's configuration and the parent's resources; protected resources are unavailable to fork pipelines; editing the fork's source needs the collaboration permission | [GL10][GL21] |

### The recommended landing

The priority order behind every instruction below is opinion shared by both researchers: truthful evidence first, the authors' signatures next, then enforced review and a check on the proposed integration, and only then the shape of the graph. The object and rule behaviour each instruction rests on is cited above.

#### Ideal, on a forge offering every method faithfully

1. **Finish the branch before approval.** Fold fix-ups, satisfy `commit-messages.md`'s rewrite rule for every commit the fold produced, re-sign where the repository signs, and parse the final messages with `git interpret-trailers --parse`. After approval the branch is published history and is never rewritten [CONV][GIT7].
2. **Choose the candidate without replay.** Pin `H` and `B`. If `B` is an ancestor of `H`, the candidate is `H` itself: an approved object whose message and signature are not touched, so anything the landing wants in its message (the pull-request URL under `Refs:`) is put on the branch before approval. Otherwise make `C = merge(B, H)` with `--no-ff`, `B` checked out as first parent, resolving any conflict explicitly and reviewing the resolution; the source objects are untouched [GIT1][EXP-A2]. A repository that wants one first-parent node per pull request may make the merge in the first case too; that is a presentation policy, not an evidence requirement (*Dissent*).
3. **Check the candidate.** Run every required gate on the candidate's tree. For `H`, that is all: the checked object lands as it is, and its own `Verified:` lines describe its tree. For a new `C`, write its message after the gate ran: Git's generated subject [CONV]; a trailer block carrying `Refs: <pull-request URL>` (or the forge's own `Reviewed-on:` where its template writes it), `Reviewed-by:` lines for the approvers, and `Verified: final contents; <gate>; <outcome>`, a claim that holds because finalising the message changes the commit's identity but not its tree [GIT11]; where the forge ran the check on the pre-finalisation candidate, cite the run by URL in the reported form (`Verified: final contents; CI run <url> on the candidate before its message was finalised; passed (reported)`), never the superseded candidate's SHA, which is no ancestor of `C` and will not stay reachable (`commit-messages.md`, *Evidence under rewrites*). Sign `C` with the landing agent's key and confirm its tree equals the tested tree. A gate that reads commit ids, parents, tags or fetched dependencies is run after finalisation, with its result kept outside the commit and bound to the final SHA; the commit is not amended again to insert it [CONV][GIT11][EXP-A3].
4. **Advance the base conditionally.** Advance to the candidate only while `B` is still the base tip and the review rules are satisfied. A plain push of a fixed candidate already fails closed on divergence [GIT3][EXP-A2]; a faithful forge queue or compare-and-swap acceptance adds the review gate. On refusal, build `C' = merge(B', C)`, check it, try again; never force the old candidate over new work, and never switch to a server replay to get past the refusal [EXP-A2].
5. **Read back.** The landed tip is the candidate and `H` is its ancestor; the branch objects and their signatures are intact.
6. **Stacks by ancestry.** When a parent lands preserved, retarget its child; the child's commits need no rewrite [EXP-F3][EXP-A3]. After a merge-commit landing the child is one commit behind (`C`), so it lands as a staged candidate in turn; after a fast-forward landing a linear stack lands by successive fast-forwards. A parent that was squashed or replayed leaves the child to be restacked deliberately with `--onto` past the old parent range [EXP-F3][EXP-A5].

Currency is not a requirement of its own: `B` need not be in the submitted branch if a checked candidate combining `B` and `H` is what lands. Currency is one way of obtaining that property, and it must not be bought by unexamined replay (mechanism, both researchers).

**Why the candidate itself is checked, not the branch.** A forge's check can run against one object, be recorded against another and be enforced against a third: GitHub's `pull_request` run checks out the temporary merge `refs/pull/N/merge`, whose `GITHUB_SHA` is the merge commit [GH7], required-status enforcement reads the test merge commit when it has statuses and the head otherwise [GH8], and a workflow may check out the head instead [GH7]; GitLab's merged results pipeline tests `refs/merge-requests/N/merge` [GL3]; Forgejo's statuses are read for the head and its default run checks out the head itself [FJ8][FJ17]. When the base moves afterwards, nothing re-runs: GitHub's `synchronize` fires on head updates only [GH22], GitLab refreshes a merged results pipeline only through a train or a pipeline created explicitly [GL3][GL5], Forgejo refreshes a counter [FJ9]. The green result then stands against a head whose eventual merge tree is `merge(B', H)`, not the `merge(B, H)` that was tested; the two share `H` and every unchanged blob and need share neither whole tree nor outcome [EXP-A1]. Landing the branch "current" closes the gap only while the base holds still between the update and the merge, and the forge rules that enforce currency are not all atomic (Forgejo's reads a queued count [FJ9]; GitHub's evaluation timing is undocumented [GH21]). Checking the candidate, and landing that exact object, is what makes the recorded result describe the landed tree.

#### GitHub in practice

**Organisation-owned repositories, where a queue is available.**

- Settings: allow merge commits only; do not enable "Require linear history"; set the merge message to the pull-request title only, never the description [GH10]. Ruleset on the integration branch: require a pull request; required status checks (the strict box may stay off, since the queue builds every group on the latest base [GH4]); "Require merge queue" with merge method **merge** and "Only merge non-failing pull requests" on; block force pushes [GH3][GH4].
- Workflows: every required check triggers on both `pull_request` and `merge_group` [GH4]. The `merge_group` run is the evidence for the group candidate. In the two observed squash landings the landed SHA carried those runs [OBS5]; the method `merge` path is not observed to share that, and a run's SHA association does not show which contents its workflow checked out [AUD-A, C030], so the landing agent reads back the SHA and tree the `merge_group` run checked and the SHA and tree that landed, treating the same SHA as the checked object landed and equal trees under different SHAs as a contents claim. The `pull_request` run on `refs/pull/N/merge` is a preview [GH7]. A local-only gate that is part of the acceptance contract reports through a trusted status on the queued candidate; a check of the old branch tip is not equivalent [GH8].
- Landing agent, after approval and a green `pull_request` run: `PUT …/pulls/{n}/merge-async` with `merge_action: merge_queue` and `sha: <approved head>`, poll the result UUID until `enqueued`, then poll the pull request until merged [GH6]; or `gh pr merge <n> --auto --match-head-commit <H>` [GH17]. A 202 or an `enqueued` state is not a landing. Never `bypass_rules` or `--admin`, which skip the queue [GH6][GH17].
- The queue-made merge commit carries GitHub's default message [GH10]; the sampled direct merge had GitHub as committer and the web-flow signature [OBS1], which a queue-made merge is not observed to share [AUD-A, C018]; the agent cannot add trailers to it. The `#N` in its subject is the retrieval handle; the evidence that its tree was checked is the `merge_group` run, bound to the landed commit where read-back shows the same SHA and to its tree otherwise [OBS5][AUD-A, C030]. Read the landed ancestry and tree back after the merge.
- Stacks: plain pull requests targeting each other's branches, head branch deleted on landing so GitHub retargets the child [GH16]; not native stacked pull requests [GH15]. If a native stack is landed by API anyway, every included open downstack pull request is approved and authorised before the top one is submitted [GH6].
- Fork pull requests take the same path; the queue landed fork pull requests in both observed cases [OBS5]; whether `merge_group` runs fork-authored workflow changes with base secrets under a given configuration is checked before the queue is enabled on a public repository.

**User-owned repositories, or where a queue is not wanted.**

- Branch protection: require a pull request; required checks with **"Require branches to be up to date before merging"**; allow merge commits only [GH2].
- Keep the branch current with **"Update with merge commit"**, never "Update with rebase" [GH14][GH1]. With the branch current, `T(merge(B, H)) = T(H)` [EXP-F1], so the `pull_request` run and the tip's own `Verified: final contents` both describe the landed tree. Each update adds a back-merge to the branch whose tree is then checked as the tip; the earlier commits' claims stay true of their own trees. Approvals the update invalidated are obtained again [GH3].
- Land with `PUT …/pulls/{n}/merge` (`merge_method: merge`, `sha: <approved head>`; 409 if the head moved, 405 if no longer mergeable) or `gh pr merge --merge --match-head-commit` [GH6][GH17]. `commit_message` carries the trailer block: `Refs:`, `Reviewed-by:` and `Verified: final contents; <gate>; <outcome>` for the gate the agent ran on a local `merge(B, H)`, whose tree the strict rule makes the landed tree; that protection is a documented policy rather than a measured guarantee, and the head guard alone is not a base guard [AUD-A, C024]. A landing without that line is not the checked landing this part prescribes.
- Friction over the ideal: every landing on a busy branch invalidates every other open pull request's currency, costing one back-merge, one check run and possibly one re-approval each. Worth paying on a low-traffic branch; a branch landing several pull requests an hour belongs in an organisation with a queue, or behind a serialising landing agent (*opinion*; no timing was measured).

**Not on GitHub:** "Rebase and merge" (rewrites trees and committers, discards signatures, cannot be re-signed [GH1][GH9]); "Squash and merge" (discards every commit but one and buries all but the last trailer block inside the body, where Git's trailer parser does not read them [EXP-F1][GIT7][OBS6]); a direct push under a pull-request rule, unless the repository has proved that route enforces the same review policy (it "may also need bypass permissions" [GH2]); granting a bot bypass to avoid one merge object (*opinion*, both researchers).

#### Forgejo in practice

- Repository: allow merge style **fast-forward-only**, and **merge** only where Fable's fork fallback (*Dissent*) has been consciously adopted; default merge style `fast-forward-only`; `DEFAULT_UPDATE_STYLE = merge` [FJ22].
- Branch protection on the integration branch: disable direct push, or whitelist only the landing agent; required status contexts naming the gates; required approvals; merge whitelist including the landing agent; "require signed commits" where the repository signs, which fast-forward-only satisfies by the hook's check of the introduced commits with no instance key needed [FJ7][FJ12]. "Block merge if pull request is outdated" is harmless but not load-bearing: fast-forward-only refuses a stale candidate by itself [FJ6].
- Landing agent, per pull request, after approval and a green run on `H`:
  1. Fetch `B` and `H`. If `B` is an ancestor of `H`, run any local gate not yet run on `H` and land `H` (step 3). Otherwise make `C = merge(B, H)` locally with `--no-ff`, `B` checked out as first parent. Run the gate on `C`. Write the message: Git's subject, then a trailer block with `Refs: <PR URL>`, `Reviewed-by:` lines for the approvers, and `Verified: final contents; <gate>; <outcome>`. Sign with the agent's SSH key.
  2. Push `C` to the pull-request branch; it is a fast-forward of `H`, since a second parent is still an ancestor [EXP-A2]. Forgejo runs the `pull_request` workflows on `C` and records statuses against it [FJ8][FJ17]. Approvals survive if the base's changes and the branch's touched different files; if a file's merged result differs from both the old head and the base, as when both sides changed it differently, the reviews are marked stale and need re-approval [FJ20][EXP-R4].
  3. `POST /repos/{o}/{r}/pulls/{i}/merge` with `Do: "fast-forward-only"`, `head_commit_id: <C or H>` and no `force_merge` [FJ13][FJ14]. The base becomes that object exactly. If Forgejo answers that the branches diverge, the base moved: build `C' = merge(B', C)` from the new base with the latest submitted head as second parent, check, push (still a fast-forward of the branch), repeat [EXP-A2]. Repeated collisions are a signal to serialise landings, never to switch to the merge or rebase style.
  4. Read back `merged_commit_id` equal to the candidate, and that the base moved (a candidate the base already contained is a no-op). Children are retargeted by the forge [FJ22]; each is now behind by the landed commit and lands by the same steps.
- Fork pull requests the agent cannot push to (no `AllowMaintainerEdit` [FJ10]): publish `C = merge(B, H)` on a maintainer-owned branch and land it by the steps above through its own pull request under the same approval rules, with the contributor's commits preserved as `C`'s second parent and the original pull request's URL in `C`'s trailers; then mark the original merged by hand with `Do: manually-merged` and `MergeCommitID` once `C` is on the base [FJ13][FJ29]. Or ask the contributor to push a current head. Where fork pull requests dominate, a landing controller that checks `merge(B, H)` and performs the native merge while holding the target still for every writer, API, UI and push alike, may cost less than staging; a process-local lock is not that controller, and the outdated rule is not one either [FJ9][AUD-A, C050] (*opinion*, both researchers). Fable's fallback, landing a current fork head with `Do: "merge"`, `head_commit_id: H` and `MergeMessageField` carrying the trailers (the API drops the default body [FJ13]; the convention tolerates the default subject's `(#N)` [CONV]), gives `T(M) = T(H)` [EXP-F1] but leaves the `CommitsBehind` window between the head check and the forge's merge [FJ9]; it is recorded under *Dissent*, not prescribed.
- Friction over the ideal: one extra check run per behind-base landing (on `C`), possible re-approval when both sides changed a file differently, and a retry loop when landings collide; a serialising landing agent removes the collisions. No forge change is needed for the mechanism. Local Git reproduced the ancestry, the candidate's push to the branch as a fast-forward, the refusal of a stale candidate and the retry [EXP-A2][EXP-R4]; the protection, status and API conclusions come from pinned source, and no end-to-end test on a live Forgejo instance was run (*Dissent*).

#### GitLab in practice

- Without a paid tier, or at modest traffic: **fast-forward method**, automatic rebase **off**, squash "Do not allow", "Pipelines must succeed", and the Forgejo procedure: stage `C` on the source branch, let the MR pipeline run on it, land with `PUT …/merge` and `sha: C`. GitLab refuses unless the candidate contains the target tip and locks the target during the fast-forward [GL13][GL15]. "Allowed to merge" suffices [GL8].
- Licensed (Premium or Ultimate) and busy: merge-commit method, squash "Do not allow", merged results pipelines, merge trains, entered with `PUT …/merge` and `auto_merge=true` [GL4][GL5]; no "merge immediately". The landed merge commit is a fresh merge whose tree matches the train's checked tree because the train serialises the target, not because it is the same object, unless the train-ref path is selected, which the pinned source allows only with the feature flag on, train skipping disallowed and the train's SHA and message matching [GL16][GL20]; read back the pipeline's SHA and tree beside the landed SHA and tree. Configure the merge template without the automatic issue-closing text, which `commit-messages.md` forbids [GL6][CONV].
- Not on GitLab: semi-linear or fast-forward *with* automatic rebase before merge, which strips signatures and re-runs no pipeline on its result [GL1]; an explicit UI or API rebase of the source, which rewrites the reviewed objects, restores no signature and may be run with `skip_ci` [GL5][GL14]; fast-forward or semi-linear trains, whose candidates are rebased [GL11]; comparing the `merge_ref` SHA with the last pipeline's SHA and then merging, since the target can move between the comparison and the merge and the API's `sha` guards the source only [GL5][AUD-A, C069].

#### The integration commit's message

Git's generated subject; no body unless the merge resolved a conflict, in which case the body describes the resolution; a trailer block with the pull-request URL under `Refs:` (a forge-written `Reviewed-on:` is kept where present), `Reviewed-by:` for the approvers, and the evidence pair for the merge's own tree [CONV]. The pull-request body is never imported: GitHub's "title and description" option, Forgejo's `${PullRequestDescription}` template variable and GitLab's description import are left off [GH10][FJ23][GL6]. Where the forge makes the commit (GitHub's queue, GitLab's train), its message is the forge's default and the agent cannot write the trailer block; that is a departure from the rule above, named as one, and the evidence lives in the forge's run record, which the landing agent reads back as a pipeline SHA and tree beside the landed SHA and tree: the same SHA where the forge landed the checked object, equal trees where it built a fresh merge of the same contents, and no evidence where neither holds [AUD-A, C030][AUD-A, C071]. Retention of that run record is a repository policy, not a given [AUD-A, C100].

### Why

Each conclusion is labelled **measurement** (observed in an experiment or a public repository), **mechanism** (documented or source-level behaviour) or **opinion**.

1. **A landing of a behind-base branch produces a tree nobody checked unless the merged result itself was checked.** Measurement, twice [EXP-F1][EXP-A1]: merge, squash, rebase and fast-forward-after-rebase gave the same final tree, different from any branch commit's; a clean merge of two separately passing sides failed its check.
2. **A rewrite costs, per commit, a re-check or a declared gap, a re-signature where the repository signs, and a rebinding; a merge commit or a fast-forward costs none of these.** Mechanism [CONV][EXP-F1][EXP-A1]: `final contents` is bound to the tree. In both fixtures a replay onto a changed base gave every replayed commit a new tree although the patch-ids were unchanged, and a replay onto a base whose tree had not changed kept the tree and lost only the signature [EXP-A3]; those are the fixtures' results, not a rule, since a content-changing base can also leave a replayed tree equal [AUD-A, C003]. The convention requires each replayed commit's claim re-established by comparison, re-run or dropped, not only the tip's, and demands no full-suite run where the tree test passes [AUD-A, C076]; identity and signature change on every replay regardless.
3. **Rewrites discard signatures and no forge restores the authors'.** Mechanism and measurement: GitHub cannot sign rebased commits [GH9] and signs squashes with its own key [OBS3]; Forgejo's replay requests no signature [FJ5]; GitLab's rebase "removes GPG signatures" [GL1]; GitHub's stack rebase "generates signed commits", GitHub's [GH15]. Merge, fast-forward and direct push keep them [EXP-F1][EXP-A1][FJ6][FJ12].
4. **Squash with a concatenated message destroys the trailer record, not only the granularity.** Measurement [EXP-F1][EXP-A3][OBS6]: concatenated messages leave one parsed trailer block; the others become body text; an appended attribution paragraph after a blank line leaves only itself parsed [GIT7]. A deliberately composed squash avoids this; the forge defaults do not.
5. **Only a landing that lands the checked object is race-free; forge currency rules are not.** Mechanism: GitHub's queue builds and checks the group head and, in the two observed squash cases, landed it as the same SHA [GH4][OBS5], other paths unobserved; Forgejo's fast-forward-only refuses unless the base is an ancestor and its push is not forced [FJ6][FJ1]; GitLab's fast-forward locks the target [GL13]. Forgejo's outdated rule reads a counter refreshed asynchronously after base pushes [FJ9], and GitHub's strict rule's evaluation timing is undocumented [GH21]. The window is small (*opinion*), but it is the difference between a guarantee and a probability. Polling the base and then calling a head-guarded merge leaves a time-of-check, time-of-use gap on every forge [GH6][FJ14][GL5].
6. **A staged candidate on Forgejo need not cost the approvals.** Measurement [EXP-R4] of the forge's own test [FJ20]: disjoint files, no stale marking; a shared file, stale marking.
7. **Merge graphs cost readers little that first-parent views do not restore; the general claim is unmeasured.** Measurement [EXP-F2][EXP-A4]: default bisect found the individual bad commit in a merged history and `--first-parent` found the landing [GIT4]; `git log --grep` found three per-commit messages in the merged history and none in the squashed one, because the squash message did not contain them; blame named the branch commit by default and the merge with `--first-parent` [GIT8]; `--first-parent` retains merge commits and hides side branches, while `--no-merges` removes merge records and with them any resolution rationale [GIT5][GIT10]. The staged-candidate shape leaves one merge commit per landing where the update-branch shape leaves two [EXP-R4]. Opinion: for readers who retrieve lexically, bisect and extract trailers, a merge graph is richer than a squashed line and equal to a rebased line; no measurement of agent retrieval quality over the two shapes exists, and default bisect in a real graph can stop at a merge interaction rather than one commit [AUD-A, C084][AUD-A, C087].
8. **A linear history earned by rebasing is the wrong trade for this audience.** Opinion resting on 2, 3 and 7; the kernel's maintainers reach the same conclusion from testing alone, treating a reparented series "like new code" to be retested, while also cautioning against gratuitous back merges [OTHER1].
9. **Test the post-merge state before it becomes the branch.** Mechanism [OTHER2]: "automatically maintain a repository of code that always passes all the tests" is what the queue, the train and the staged candidate do; passing finite checks is a gate, not a correctness theorem [AUD-A, C099].
10. **Stacks favour preserved landings and penalise native GitHub stacks.** Measurement [EXP-F3][EXP-A3][EXP-A5]: after a merge or fast-forward landing the child needed no rewrite, though after a merge it is behind the new integration commit; after a squash the child's plain rebase conflicted and `--onto` past the old parent was needed; after a rebase the child was rewritten with the same tree and a new hash. Mechanism [GH15]: a native GitHub stack is rebased at every landing and whenever the trunk moves.
11. **Signatures should survive where the repository signs.** Opinion: a signature binds a commit payload to a key under the repository's trust policy and is the strongest binding of a `Verified:` claim to its maker; a forge re-signature attests that the forge made the object. A signature proves neither that a test ran nor that code is benign, and unsigned author fields and external attestations also record identity, with less strength [AUD-A, C097]. The recommendation holds in unsigned repositories on evidence truth and record granularity alone.

### Alternatives and when they would win

- **Always a merge commit, even when the head contains the base.** Wins where one first-parent node per pull request is a declared reader requirement, where the merge commit is the wanted carrier for the pull-request URL and reviewer record without touching a reviewed commit, or where native queues and fork handling save more than the extra object costs. The GitHub practical default is this shape because GitHub offers nothing else. Its cost is a redundant object and, on Forgejo, one extra check run per landing (*Dissent*).
- **Fast-forward of the checked tip without a merge commit, bors-style when the base moved** (rebase locally, re-check and re-sign every commit, then true fast-forward). Wins under a hard linear-history requirement, for a linear stack landing in sequence, and when commits are few and gates fast. Loses whenever the base moves between review and landing: every commit rewritten, re-checked, re-signed, possibly re-approved, and its pre-rebase evidence dropped under `commit-messages.md`'s ancestor rule. GitHub's server rebase is not this: it always rewrites [GH1].
- **Checked candidate pushed directly to the base** by a landing controller with push rights. Wins on Forgejo and GitLab where the project accepts a push-whitelisted bot and has proved the controller enforces review itself; integrates forks without writing their branches; needs the pull request's merged state handled by hand [FJ29]. On GitHub it may need bypass permission under a pull-request rule [GH2]. Never justified merely by discovering that an admin token can do it (*opinion*, both researchers).
- **Merge style with the outdated rule and update-by-merge** (Fable's round-one Forgejo answer). Fable's position for fork pull requests the agent cannot push to, acceptable only where the `CommitsBehind` window is consciously accepted; loses the race-freedom, the agent's signature and the message control of the staged candidate, and leaves two merge commits per behind-base landing [FJ9][FJ13][EXP-R4].
- **One deliberately composed squash.** Wins only if the pull request, not the commit, were the unit of record, or where the branch's commits are disposable work logs; the convention anticipates it ("where the branch will land by squash, the rebinding goes into the composed message" [CONV]) but its evidence model is per commit, and a pasted review brief or an automatic concatenation is not a composed record [EXP-A3]. A repository wanting one commit per pull request folds to one commit *before* review and lands it preserved.
- **Rebase-merge** (Forgejo `rebase-merge`). Inherits every rewrite cost and adds a merge commit; wins nowhere here.
- **Loose merge with historical branch checks only.** Preserves commit claims and lands an unchecked tree; acceptable only where post-merge discovery is consciously accepted for low-risk work, labelled as such and never called evidence-equivalent to a queue (both researchers).

Things one researcher found that the other missed, carried above: GitHub's native stacked pull requests and the retarget-on-deletion behaviour [GH15][GH16], the direct-push bypass sentence [GH2] and Forgejo's combined-diff stale-review test [FJ20][EXP-R4] (Fable's judgement); the asynchronous merge endpoint [GH6], the `CommitsBehind` refresh path [FJ9][FJ27][FJ28], the resolution of Forgejo's auto-merge protection question [FJ16], the tree-neutral base case and the message-finalisation point [EXP-A3] (Astra's audit). Fable's round-one claims the audit found wrong and that are not carried: that the merge tree equals the head tree "if and only if" the head contains the base; that a rebase onto any moved base necessarily changes every tree; that `--ff-only` always refuses a behind branch; that no Forgejo style but merge keeps signatures; that Forgejo never checks a merge; that GitLab's semi-linear and fast-forward methods require a rebase; that a `merge_ref` comparison closes the race; that `--first-parent` hides merge commits; that the queue is the only mechanism that lands a checked object; that the convention has no claim able to describe a checked merge [AUD-A][AUD-F]. Two of the audit's own findings do not stand and are not carried either: the convention does forbid an issue or pull-request number in an authored subject while a native merge subject keeps its number [CONV], and the merge-queue page does say the queue "provides the same benefits as the Require branches to be up to date before merging branch protection" (read 11 October 2026).

**Uncertainties both researchers record.**

- GitHub's strict currency rule: whether "Require branches to be up to date" is evaluated against a fresh base at merge time or against cached mergeability is undocumented [GH21]; the queue sidesteps it, the no-queue practice does not.
- GitHub's queue with method "merge" was not observed; the Bevy observation used squash. The mechanism does not depend on the method; rated strong but unverified for "merge" [OBS5].
- The Forgejo staged-candidate procedure was derived from source and reproduced in local Git [EXP-A2][EXP-R4], not run against a live instance; the status attachment, the stale-review behaviour and the fast-forward refusal are what an hour on a test instance would confirm.
- Forgejo's `pull_request_target` context: documentation and pinned source disagree for a non-default target [FJ24][FJ17].
- Forgejo's rebase-template amend path: whether ambient configuration signs it is unverified [FJ4]; not a route prescribed here.
- GitLab trains in merge-commit mode land a fresh merge whose tree equals the checked tree only because the train serialises the target; "merge immediately" breaks that [GL4][GL16].
- The `CommitsBehind` window on Forgejo is real but unmeasured; it is the goroutine's latency after a base push [FJ9].
- Forge run records as durable evidence: neither researcher established retention or archival access for the runs cited; a SHA association is evidence, durability needs a policy [AUD-A, C100].
- No published measurement of agent retrieval over merge graphs against linear histories exists.

**Premises of the brief that both researchers revised, and that this record adopts.**

1. *"Nothing in the pull-request title and body is meant to enter history."* Partly wrong: the body is a review brief and should not enter history; the pull request's identity should, because readers retrieve by it, the forges put it in merge subjects [GH10][FJ1][GL6] and the convention expects a forge-appended `(#N)` [CONV]. The integration commit's trailer block carries it, and any new integration decision is described in that commit's body; the mutable review brief is never imported.
2. *"The commits are the record"* left the landed tree without a claim: a merge that resolves nothing "needs no body" [CONV], yet its tree can fail a check both sides passed [EXP-A1]. The landing agent or queue binds evidence to the integration commit; `Verified: final contents` already describes a merge's tree, so no new claim type is needed [AUD-A, C095]; landed history is never amended to add a later result.
3. *Tree equality is a contents test, not execution equivalence.* A check that reads commit ids, parents, tags or fetched dependencies can differ on identical trees; record those inputs or re-run. Measured only that same-tree, different-commit cases exist [EXP-A3]; effects on real builds unverified.
4. *"The tested commit's hash need not stay reachable"* [CONV] is truthful but not durable: unreachable objects are collected [GIT9], so a rebound claim can point at a hash no one can inspect. Both researchers raised it independently; `commit-messages.md` now cites a commit only when it is an ancestor, and the finding further favours landings that keep the tested commits on the base's ancestry.
5. *"Weigh a preference for linear history on its merits."* Accepted, and the weighing goes against it (Why 7 and 8).
6. *"Whether signatures must survive is yours to weigh."* They should survive where the repository signs (Why 11); every rewrite method on every forge silently discards them [GH9][OBS3][FJ5][GL1][GH15].
7. *"Writers land several pull requests an hour."* Accepted as the premise that makes a queue or a serialising landing agent the strong recommendation on a busy branch; the rate itself was not measured and does not by itself prove necessity.

### What would change the answer

- A measurement showing per-commit re-checking of a rebased branch is cheap for the gates in use would make the bors-style fast-forward alternative competitive; its remaining costs would be re-signing and the dropped pre-rebase evidence.
- A GitHub fast-forward-only merge method that lands the checked merge-group head under the same rules, or merge queues for personal repositories, would remove the no-queue currency treadmill and the redundant merge object.
- A Forgejo integration queue, or an expected-base parameter on its merge endpoint, would make the merge style race-free and the easier default for forks.
- Evidence that GitHub's queue with method "merge" lands a different SHA from the one it checked, or that a stale queue entry can land after its base moved, would downgrade the GitHub recommendation.
- A finding that Forgejo's `pull_request` statuses do not attach to a merge-commit head, or that the stale-review test behaves otherwise than measured, would weaken the staged-candidate route.
- GitLab making train-ref acceptance uniform and documented would strengthen the exact-object claim for trains; fast-forward and semi-linear trains would also have to stop replaying their candidates.
- Measured candidate retries, gate duration, approval churn, fork frequency and evidence-repair effort could favour always-merge or a simpler exclusive controller over staging; no such comparison has been run.
- A controlled agent and human retrieval study over fixed histories (original graph, faithful linear replay, composed squash), scoring evidence recovery and answer accuracy rather than lexical hits, could overturn the opinion that preserving retrieval units outweighs graph simplification.
- A convention change making the pull request the unit of record would make squash the natural landing; a requirement that contributors' exact signatures survive would rule out every server rewrite even where trees match.
- GitHub documenting that native stacks preserve commit objects when the parent lands by merge commit would remove the objection to them.
- **Gitea coverage**, an outstanding requirement of the brief: version-scoped findings on Gitea's merge styles, protection rules, status lookup and merge API from its own documentation and pinned source, with a practical route; until then this part answers for Forgejo only.

## Method

A dated log. The body describes only the current position; what it used to say, and why it changed, is recorded here.

### 5 October 2026: the pull-request body

Two rounds, each with one `claude-fable-5-1` worker and one `gpt-6-astra` worker at `high` effort, working blind in separate directories, under a brief that fixed three premises: branches land by rebase and fast-forward; commit messages in the format of `commit-messages.md` are the record; the pull request is the place for review and still needs a body. The target forge was GitHub, with Forgejo's behaviour noted where it differs.

1. **Proposals.** Each designer answered the same brief independently. The brief set aside an earlier design (3 October 2026), made for squash and merge-commit landing, that turned the body into a mechanical copy of the commit message; under fast-forward that copy controlled nothing, and it made the redundant part mandatory and the review material optional. *What was rejected* describes it.
2. **Judgement and synthesis.** Each designer read its own proposal, then the other's, judged the other's against the brief while fetching the sources behind its factual claims, and then wrote a synthesis of both. Astra rated its own proposal stronger; Fable rated the two even. Astra's checks found five of Fable's claims wrong or overstated; Fable's confirmed Astra's. Each synthesis kept its author's section structure while adopting most of the other's content.

Part one is the Fable synthesis, chosen for its concrete sections, a body with nothing that goes stale on a rebase, and its consistency with the commit format, which keeps results in `Verified:` trailers. It was edited after the round in three ways: `Check` was made conditional rather than required, following Astra's judgement that a required section invites invented doubts; the GitHub landing route was corrected against GitHub's documentation (a merge queue builds its own commits, so it cannot fast-forward; landing was then a push of the approved tip by an identity allowed to bypass branch protection; stale approvals are dismissed only by pushes that affect the diff; the fork condition was added); references to the earlier design were made self-contained, and every remaining Fable claim that Astra found wrong or overstated was corrected. The edited report was not cross-checked again. The proposals, judgements, the Astra synthesis and the run records are not included; every claim cites its primary source directly. Confidence was recorded as moderate: no study compares pull-request body formats. A one-screen distillation was written beside it as `GUIDANCE-DRAFT.md`; the repository's `guidance/pull-requests.md` descends from it.

Open points recorded at the time: pull requests from forks (GitHub records a fast-forward as a merge only when the pull request's own head commits reach the base, so a rebase or reword had to reach the contributor's branch first, and outside contributors' messages would not follow the format; how a repository with many outside contributions lands them was undecided); the landing identity (pushing to a protected branch needed an identity allowed to bypass protection, so the landing step had to check approval and passing checks on the exact tip itself); and benefit and length, unmeasured (a scored sample of agent-written bodies would show whether the sections steer agents away from filler and invented results).

### 11 October 2026: the landing research, and the retitling of this record

**How it ran.** Two researchers, `gpt-6-astra` and `claude-fable-5-1`, each at `xhigh` effort, answered one brief blind: what each landing method does per forge, which preserves the record, whether currency is required, readability afterwards, an ideal and a practical answer, and which premises to challenge. The brief supplied only `commit-messages.md` and forbade reading this project's own guidance or research on landing, so that neither answer was anchored on the existing decision. Each researcher fetched vendor documentation and pinned forge source (Forgejo v16.0.5 at `8d24b7d5…`, GitLab v19.4.1-ee at `26212baa…`), observed public repositories through the forges' APIs, and ran local Git 2.56.0 experiments. In round two each read the other's report, re-fetched every cited source, re-ran or reproduced the other's experiments, graded the other's claims, and wrote a synthesis. Astra's audit graded 100 of Fable's claims: 57 confirmed, 14 wrong, 22 overstated, 7 unverifiable. Fable's audit graded 75 of Astra's: none wrong, one overstated by its citation only, one unverifiable and labelled so; Fable rated Astra's report the stronger and adopted its Forgejo and GitLab answers. Both syntheses agreed on the ideal's shape (preserve the reviewed objects; check the integration; land the checked object), on GitHub (merge commits, queue where available, strict currency otherwise), on Forgejo (staged fast-forward candidate; for fork heads the agent cannot push to, Astra requires a maintainer-owned integration pull request or an exclusive controller while Fable keeps the merge style with a disclosed window, and the record follows Astra) and on GitLab (prepared fast-forward, trains when licensed). Where they differ, *Dissent* records both positions and prefers the one the audits support. Part two was written from both syntheses and both judgements by the editor of this record; no third researcher checked it.

**What changed, and why.**

- **The landing premise.** Earlier position (Part one's brief and body, 5 October): branches land by rebase and fast-forward; before landing, fold the fix-ups, reword, rebase onto the target, push once with a `git diff` statement, then fast-forward; on GitHub, a push of the approved tip by an identity allowed to bypass branch protection. New position: never rewrite reviewed commits; the branch is finished and folded before approval and not rebased; the approved head lands as the second parent of a checked integration candidate whose first parent is the base, built and checked before landing and then the base advanced to exactly it; when the head already contains the base, fast-forward to the head itself (Astra), with always-merge recorded as Fable's position and as a repository policy a project may declare. Basis: both researchers' experiments showing that a rebase onto a changed base gave every commit in their fixtures a new tree and loses every commit's signature, with tree equality to be established per commit rather than assumed, that every landing of a behind-base branch produces a tree no branch commit was checked on, and that a clean merge of two passing sides can fail [EXP-F1][EXP-A1]; GitHub's documentation that a direct push under a pull-request rule "may also need bypass permissions" [GH2]; the audits' resolution of the merge-when-eligible question [AUD-A, C090][AUD-F]. Part one's *Finishing the branch, and landing*, the forge-differences paragraph on GitHub, worked example C's landing comment, *What was rejected*, *What would change the answer* and the *Origins* table were revised to the current position; the title, which named the earlier premise, was changed.
- **"Land the branch current so the merge commit's tree is the tip the checks ran on."** Earlier position (the repository's landing guidance distilled from the 5 October research): keep the branch current so that the merge commit's tree is the tip the checks ran on. New position: check the candidate itself, the object that will land. Basis: the staleness mechanism in *The recommended landing*: a forge's check can run against a temporary merge of the head into the base as it then stood, be recorded or enforced against the head or that test merge, and is not re-run when the base moves, and the currency rules that would close the gap are not all atomic (Forgejo's reads a queued count; GitHub's timing is undocumented) [GH7][GH8][GH22][GL3][FJ8][FJ9][GH21][EXP-A1][AUD-A, C050]. Currency remains the no-queue GitHub practice, by merge and never by rebase, because GitHub offers no way to land a prepared object through its pull-request rules.
- **Two of the 5 October open points closed.** Forks: Part two gives a route per forge (the queue on GitHub; on Forgejo a maintainer-owned integration pull request, with the merge style for a current fork head recorded under *Dissent*; GitLab's collaboration permission). The landing identity: the merge whitelist or "Allowed to merge" lands through the pull request on Forgejo and GitLab, fast-forward-only needs no bypass and no instance key, and on GitHub the queue lands under the rules; a direct push remains a configured route that must prove its own review enforcement. Benefit and length of the body remain unmeasured.
